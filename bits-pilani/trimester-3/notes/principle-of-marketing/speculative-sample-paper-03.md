## Q.1

### Q.1.1 — Earned, Owned, and Paid Media (5 Marks)

(a) Distinguish between **earned media**, **owned media**, and **paid media** in the digital marketing mix. Explain how the three work together, and why over-reliance on paid media alone is a risk.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

| Media type | Definition | Examples |
|------------|------------|----------|
| **Earned media** | Publicity gained organically through reputation, word-of-mouth, and third-party mentions — *not purchased* | Customer reviews, social shares, press coverage, unpaid influencer mentions |
| **Owned media** | Digital assets the brand **fully controls** | Website, blog, social profiles, email lists, app |
| **Paid media** | Marketing efforts that require **payment** to promote content | PPC/search ads, display banners, social ads, remarketing |

**How they compare.**

| Dimension | Earned | Owned | Paid |
|-----------|--------|-------|------|
| Cost | Free (organic) | Platform maintenance | Per impression/click |
| Control | Low | High | Medium (platform rules) |
| Credibility | **Highest** | High | Lower |
| Speed | Slow to build | Medium | Fast |

**How they work together.** Owned media is the **foundation** (traffic must land somewhere credible); paid media is the **catalyst** (buy reach, drive traffic to owned assets, spark conversation); earned media is the **credibility layer** (satisfied customers generate organic buzz). The loop compounds: paid → owned → earned → more paid opportunities.

**Risk of paid-only.** Over-reliance on paid without owned/earned gives **expensive, low-trust growth**: paid traffic lands nowhere credible, and growth stops the moment the budget stops. Sustainable growth needs all three balanced.

**Pro tip:** The classic trap is confusing earned with owned — a brand's Instagram post is **owned**; a *customer's share* of that post is **earned**. Give one example per type, then one sentence on the compound loop (paid sparks, owned converts, earned builds trust). If asked which is most credible, the answer is always **earned** — peer recommendations beat ads.

### Q.1.2 — AIDA and the Funnel-to-Flywheel Shift (5 Marks)

(b) Explain the **AIDA** model and its four stages. Then contrast the classic **marketing funnel** with the **flywheel** mindset, explaining what changes after the purchase stage in the flywheel view.

#### Answer

**AIDA** is the classic attention-to-action framework:

| Stage | Goal | Tactics |
|-------|------|---------|
| **Attention** | Get noticed; first recognition | Bold visuals, striking headlines, contrast |
| **Interest** | Keep the audience curious | Provocative questions, surprising answers |
| **Desire** | Turn curiosity into emotional need | Storytelling, value promises, sensory experience |
| **Action** | Drive the next step | CTA, contact info, purchase, visit |

Modern practice adds two **pre-stages** before AIDA: **proving** (build credibility via reviews, portfolios, case studies) and **priming** (build cultural relevance and emotional connection through repeated exposure).

**Funnel vs flywheel.**

| | Funnel | Flywheel |
|---|--------|----------|
| Journey end | Action (purchase) | Continuous momentum |
| Focus | Linear conversion | Retention, advocacy, compounding value |

In the **funnel** view, marketing "ends" at purchase. The **flywheel** extends AIDA beyond action with two post-purchase stages — **Confidence** (trust that the purchase was right) and **Satisfaction** (post-purchase delight) — which feed back into the top of the funnel as retention, referrals, and advocacy. Satisfaction loops back into Attention for new customers, making growth compound rather than leak.

**Pro tip:** AIDA questions reward a 4-row table with tactics, plus the punchline that digital touchpoints can hit several stages *simultaneously* (AIDA is not strictly sequential in one medium). For funnel-vs-flywheel, the differentiator is one sentence: *the funnel ends at purchase; the flywheel adds confidence and satisfaction and loops them back in*. Trap: ending the journey at Action is funnel thinking — flywheel thinking requires the post-purchase loop.

## Q.2

### Q.2.1 — Cost-Based vs Value-Based Pricing (5 Marks)

(a) A new electric scooter company must set its price. Contrast **cost-based pricing** with **value-based pricing**, including the sequence of decisions each implies. Recommend which approach fits a premium e-scooter brand and justify with the pricing-floor/ceiling logic.

#### Answer

**The pricing zone.** Every workable price sits between a **floor** (product costs — below which profit vanishes) and a **ceiling** (customer-perceived value — above which buyers walk away). Competition and strategy fill the zone between.

**Cost-based vs value-based.**

| Aspect | Cost-based | Value-based |
|--------|------------|-------------|
| Starting point | Product and internal cost | Customer needs and perceived value |
| Sequence | Product → cost → price → convince buyers of value | Perceived value → target price → allowable cost → design product to deliver |
| Driver | Internal cost structure | Customer expectations and perceived value |
| Direction | Company → consumer | Consumer → company |
| Fit | Commodities, cost-visible markets (Maruti Alto) | Premium, differentiated brands (Porsche) |

**Recommendation: value-based pricing** for a premium e-scooter brand. The floor/ceiling logic decides it: the cost-based route would price from components upward and then struggle to justify the number; value-based pricing starts from what the target buyer's perceived value will bear (design, range, brand, status), sets the target price at that ceiling-aware level, and works backward to an *allowable cost* the product must be designed to meet. Because the premium segment is less price-sensitive (more inelastic), value-based pricing captures the surplus that cost-plus would leave on the table.

**Pro tip:** Pricing questions with "recommend one approach" want the **floor/ceiling diagram logic** in words: cost sets the floor, perceived value sets the ceiling, and your strategy picks where in the zone to sit. The trap is reversing the sequences — cost-based starts from the product, value-based starts from perceived value. If a question asks about *competition-based* pricing instead, remember it can price above, below, or at parity — and warn about price wars in homogeneous markets.

### Q.2.2 — Market Skimming vs Penetration Pricing (5 Marks)

(b) A pharma startup launches a patented breakthrough drug, while a streaming platform launches in a price-sensitive new market. Contrast **market skimming** with **penetration pricing**, and justify which company should use which.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

| Dimension | Market skimming | Market penetration |
|-----------|-----------------|--------------------|
| Initial price | **High** | **Low** |
| Primary aim | Maximise early surplus — skim successive segments from high WTP down | Win share and scale quickly; deter rivals |
| Later move | Price steps down over time | Often price steps up after lock-in/habit forms |
| Conditions for success | Strong high-quality image; enough early adopters willing to pay now | High price sensitivity; scale and network effects matter |

**Justification.**

- **Pharma startup → skimming.** A patented breakthrough drug has no direct substitutes (high WTP early adopters), strong perceived value, and patent protection that blocks rivals. The firm skims the surplus from those who value it most, then steps the price down as patents expire and competition arrives. Without rivals, there is no share to win by underpricing.
- **Streaming platform → penetration.** A price-sensitive mass market, aggressive existing competition, and a business that depends on scale and habit formation. A low/free intro price acquires millions of users; economies of scale and switching costs then allow the platform to raise prices after lock-in.

**Pro tip:** This pairing is a classic "match strategy to company" question — answer with a contrast table plus one justification line per company anchored to *rivalry and price sensitivity*. Traps: using skimming without a quality image or early adopters (the high opening price fails), and confusing the two (skimming starts high and steps down; penetration starts low and often steps up). If asked for a class example, smartphones = skimming; streaming/low-cost retail = penetration.

## Q.3

### Q.3.1 — Brand Architecture Pyramid (5 Marks)

(a) Explain the **brand architecture pyramid** and its levels from base to peak. Using one of IndiGo, Apple, or Disney, show how a brand climbs from tangible features to an intangible brand essence.

#### Answer

**The brand architecture pyramid** organises brand identity from the concrete to the ethereal. At the base sit tangible features; at the peak sits intangible brand essence. Strong brands climb the pyramid coherently so customers remember not just *what the product does* but *what the brand means*.

| Level (bottom → top) | Nature | Role |
|----------------------|--------|------|
| **Features & attributes** | Tangible | What the product *has* |
| **Functional / product benefits** | Practical | What it *does* / delivers |
| **Emotional benefits** | Feeling | How it makes the customer *feel* |
| **Brand personality** | Character | Human traits of the brand |
| **Brand core / essence** | Meaning | Enduring idea / positioning rally cry |

**Example — IndiGo Airlines:**

| Level | Content |
|-------|---------|
| Features & benefits | On-time performance, courteous staff, spotless interiors |
| Functional benefits | Low-cost airline with high performance |
| Emotional benefits | Affordable **and dependable** |
| Brand core / essence | Sincere yet fun; making the dream of every Indian flying come true |

**Why it matters.** The pyramid forces clarity from features → meaning, prevents "stuck-at-features" branding, builds consumer connection through emotion and essence, and keeps campaigns consistent. A brand that only advertises features is stuck at the base; a brand that claims "magical" without delivering at the base feels hollow.

**Pro tip:** A pyramid question is a 5-level list + one worked brand. Use *one* brand (mixing IndiGo/Apple/Disney details is a designed trap). The trap to flag: confusing **functional benefits** ("low cost") with **emotional benefits** ("dependable — a feeling") — functional is practical, emotional is how it feels. Also do not confuse the architecture pyramid (identity structure) with the brand *development grid* (extension strategies) — different tools.

### Q.3.2 — Copywriting and the ABC Behaviour Model (5 Marks)

(b) Distinguish **copywriting** from **content writing**. Then, using the **ABC behaviour model**, explain how a marketer would diagnose and fix the problem: *"visitors arrive on the website but do not buy."*

#### Answer

**Copywriting vs content writing.**

| Dimension | Copywriting | Content writing |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Goal | Immediate action — purchase, click, subscribe | Inform, educate, build trust |
| Tone | Persuasive, punchy, urgent | Educational, narrative |
| Length | Short — headlines, ads, CTAs, landing hero | Longer — blogs, white papers, case studies |
| Success metric | Conversion / response | Reach, time-on-page, trust |

Content builds the relationship; copy closes the moment.

**ABC behaviour model.** Every behaviour has three parts:

- **A — Antecedents:** triggers immediately before the behaviour.
- **B — Behaviour:** the observable action.
- **C — Consequences:** outcomes that follow and often reinforce the behaviour.

**Diagnosing "visitors arrive but don't buy":** the traffic exists, so attention (A) is working. The failure is therefore most likely in **B — Behaviour**: friction in the act itself. Interventions should cut friction: clearer UX, simplified checkout, pricing clarity, trust signals, fewer form fields. (If people *didn't know* the brand, the gap would be A — weak antecedents; if they *bought once but never returned*, the gap would be C — weak consequences/rewards.)

**Applying the model to fix the problem:** make the behaviour easier (one-click order, clear CTA, minimal steps), pair it with a reinforcing consequence (loyalty points, confirmation, immediate value), so the loop becomes trigger → easy action → reward — the Starbucks pattern of push notification → reorder → skip the line + points.

**Pro tip:** ABC questions are about *diagnosing which stage is broken* — match the symptom to the stage: awareness gaps = A, friction = B, no return/repeat = C. Never answer "better creative" for a B problem — more ads flood antecedents but don't fix checkout friction. If the question blends copy and content, the one-liner that earns marks is: *copy converts the moment, content builds the relationship over time*.

## Q.4

### Q.4.1 — Marketing ROS and MER Calculation (5 Marks)

(a) Define **Net Marketing Contribution (NMC)**, **Marketing ROS**, and **MER**. A company has net sales of ₹1,50,000, COGS of ₹90,000, and marketing expenses of ₹50,000. Calculate the NMC and Marketing ROS, and explain what each number means. State any assumption made.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

$$\text{NMC} = \text{Net sales} - \text{COGS} - \text{Marketing expenses}$$

$$\text{Marketing ROS} = \frac{\text{NMC}}{\text{Net sales}}$$

$$\text{MER} = \frac{\text{Total revenue}}{\text{Total advertising spend}}$$

**Calculation.**

| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Net sales | ₹1,50,000 |
| COGS | ₹90,000 |
| Marketing expenses | ₹50,000 |
| NMC | $1{,}50{,}000 - 90{,}000 - 50{,}000 = ₹10{,}000$ |
| Marketing ROS | $10{,}000 / 1{,}50{,}000 \approx 0.0667 = 6.7\%$ |

**Interpretation.**

- **NMC of ₹10,000** is the profit attributable to marketing after covering goods sold and marketing spend — the contribution the marketing activity genuinely added.
- **Marketing ROS of ~6.7%** means about 6.7% of net sales becomes marketing-generated profit. It is the metric that separates two campaigns with *equal revenue but different spend*: the one with the higher ROS contributes more profit and should win the budget.
- **MER** (not computable from the given data) is the system-wide efficiency lens — total revenue per ad rupee (a healthy band is often ~3–4) — useful when precise campaign attribution is hard.

**Assumption:** the stated COGS and marketing expenses are the full, correctly matched costs for the period — i.e., no hidden costs are excluded.

**Pro tip:** ROS questions are calculations — show NMC first (never divide "profit" directly), then ROS, and box the answer. The traps: confusing ROS with ROAS (return on ad spend — different numerator/denominator), and treating MER and ROS as synonyms (MER is revenue/ad spend; ROS is contribution/sales). If the question gives two campaigns with equal sales, the mark is for saying ROS reveals which is *efficient on profit*, not which sold more.

### Q.4.2 — Market Share and Sales vs Marketing (5 Marks)

(b) (i) Calculate the **market share** of a company with sales of ₹80 crore in an industry whose total sales are ₹400 crore. Then compute its **relative market share** if the largest competitor's sales are ₹100 crore, and interpret the result. (ii) Briefly explain the relationship between the marketing and sales functions, and give one KPI owned by each.

#### Answer

**(i) Market share calculations.**

$$\text{Market share}_{\text{revenue}} = \frac{\text{Company revenue}}{\text{Total industry revenue}} \times 100 = \frac{80}{400} \times 100 = 20\%$$

$$\text{Relative market share} = \frac{\text{Company sales}}{\text{Largest competitor's sales}} = \frac{80}{100} = 0.8$$

**Interpretation.** The company holds **20% of the industry's revenue** — a significant slice. But its relative market share of **0.8 < 1** means it is a **follower** — behind the market leader, which is larger. Relative share is the BCG-style lens: it compares against the #1 rival, not the whole industry. The strategic reading: decent absolute size, but the leader still has the scale advantage.

**(ii) Sales vs marketing.**

**Relationship:** marketing is the broader system; sales is a promotional, action-oriented arm *within* it. Marketing **creates and nurtures demand** (awareness, segmentation, pipeline); sales **converts that demand into purchases** (personalised outreach, demos, closing). Marketing has the longer horizon (traffic, visibility, MQLs); sales has the shorter cycle (SQLs, deals closed, revenue).

| Function | Example KPI |
|----------|-------------|
| **Marketing** | MQLs, traffic, visibility, pipeline generation |
| **Sales** | SQLs, deals closed, revenue |

**Pro tip:** Market-share calculations are pure arithmetic — state the formula, show the numbers, and interpret the result (a number alone is worth half the marks). Traps: mixing revenue and unit bases in one comparison, and confusing absolute share (vs industry) with relative share (vs leader). For the sales-vs-marketing half, the one-liner is *marketing creates demand, sales converts it*; the KPI distinction (MQLs = marketing, SQLs/closed revenue = sales) is the classic trap.
