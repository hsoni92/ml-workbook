## Q.1

### Q.1.1 — Need, Want, and Demand (6 Marks)

(a) Distinguish between a "Need", a "Want", and "Demand" in marketing. Explain why a marketer can *shape wants* but *cannot create needs*, and describe the conditions under which a want is converted into demand.

#### Answer

**The three terms are distinct but sequential.**

| Term | Nature | Example | Marketer's role |
|------|--------|---------|-----------------|
| **Need** | Fundamental requirement for survival/function; universal and stable | Food, water, shelter, clothing | **Cater** — provide options that fulfil it (marketers do *not* create needs) |
| **Want** | Specific desire for a product, shaped by culture, trends, ads, peers | Sushi, Nike sneakers, an iPhone | **Create and influence** via branding, positioning, lifestyle association, storytelling |
| **Demand** | A want backed by **ability + willingness to pay** | iPhone purchase when the buyer can afford it | **Convert** through pricing and perceived value |

**Why marketers cannot create needs.** Needs are biological/functional necessities that exist independently of marketing. Competition is about *how* the need is met, not *whether* it exists — everyone needs food, but marketers differ on the solution (fast food vs meal kits). What marketing *can* do is convert a generic need into a **specific want**: the need for food becomes a want for a particular brand or format. This is the classic trap distinction — "marketers create needs" is **wrong**; they create wants.

**From want to demand.** A want only becomes demand when three conditions hold simultaneously:

$$\text{Demand} = \text{Need/Want} + \text{Ability to pay} + \text{Willingness to pay}$$

Many people *want* a latest iPhone; demand exists only among those who can afford it and believe the price is justified. A campaign that generates buzz (want) but ignores affordability will not create demand. **Pricing and perceived value** are the two levers that convert desire into transactions.

**Pro tip:** Any "need-want-demand" question is a three-row table in disguise. Write the table with *nature, example, marketer's role*, then one sentence on the conversion condition: *want + ability + willingness = demand*. The trap to call out explicitly: marketing creates **wants**, not needs. If the question gives an example like "a customer wants a car but cannot afford it," label it correctly as a want without demand.

### Q.1.2 — Tangible vs Intangible Value (4 Marks)

(b) Distinguish between tangible and intangible value, giving one example of each. Explain why a brand that competes mainly on intangible value tends to have **more inelastic demand**.

#### Answer

**Tangible value** = objective, measurable, logical benefits; easy to quantify.
**Intangible value** = subjective, emotional, psychological benefits; harder to quantify but often decisive.

| Dimension | Tangible value | Intangible value |
|-----------|----------------|------------------|
| Sub-types | **Economic** (price/efficiency) + **Functional** (utility/performance) | **Social** (status, identity, belonging) + **Experiential** (sensory/emotional satisfaction) |
| Consumer question | "Is this a good investment?" / "Does this do the job?" | "What will people think of me?" / "How does it feel?" |
| Example | A car with 25 km/l fuel efficiency; a phone with 48-hour battery | A Rolex as a status symbol; Starbucks ambience and brand cup |
| Marketing lever | Feature comparison, pricing | Storytelling, experience design, brand identity |

**Why intangible value creates inelastic demand.** Products heavy on intangible/experiential value (Starbucks coffee vs generic coffee) command **loyalty beyond price** — consumers stay attached even when the price rises, because the emotional/identity payoff is not substitutable. Buyers compare less on spec sheets and more on meaning, so a price increase does not cause a proportionally large drop in quantity demanded — that is the definition of **inelastic demand**. This is exactly why brands invest in experience and identity beyond functional specs: it buys pricing power.

**Pro tip:** Pairing tangible/intangible with elasticity is a favourite combo. Use the two sub-type lists (economic + functional vs social + experiential), one example per side, then the pricing-power punchline: *strong intangible value → substitutes feel weaker → demand is more inelastic*. Don't confuse **social** value (how others see me) with **experiential** value (how I feel using it) — that is a designed trap.

## Q.2

### Q.2.1 — BCG Growth-Share Matrix (6 Marks)

(a) Explain the BCG Growth-Share Matrix, its two axes, and the four quadrants. For each quadrant, state the recommended strategic action and give a product example.

#### Answer

**Purpose.** The BCG matrix classifies a company's products (or business units) on **relative market share** (x-axis) and **market growth rate** (y-axis), so investment can be allocated selectively — not equally — across the portfolio.

| Quadrant | Market share | Market growth | Recommended action | Example |
|----------|--------------|---------------|--------------------|---------|
| **Star** | High | High | **Invest heavily** — defend leadership; future cash generator | Category-leading energy drink in a growing market |
| **Cash Cow** | High | Low | **Milk / harvest** — maximise margin, reinvest cash elsewhere | Leading detergent in a mature market |
| **Question Mark** | Low | High | **Selective investment or exit** — decide if it can become a star | New plant-based snack in a growing category |
| **Dog** | Low | Low | **Divest, reposition, or phase out** | Declining legacy product in a shrinking segment |

**Portfolio logic.** Cash cows fund the stars and the *chosen* question marks; stars mature into cash cows; question marks that fail become dogs. The portfolio must balance cash generation with growth bets.

**Limitations to mention for full marks:** the matrix is a **static 2×2 snapshot** — it ignores profitability, synergies, and strategic fit; share and growth depend on how the *market boundary* is drawn; and products move quadrants as markets evolve, so it needs periodic reassessment.

**Pro tip:** BCG questions reward (1) naming both axes exactly, (2) the four-row quadrant table with action + example, and (3) one limitation. The signature traps: calling a low-share/high-growth product a "star" (it is a question mark), treating cash cows as growth investments (they are harvested), and keeping dogs out of sentiment (they drain resources). Remember the quadrant placement for any diagram you draw: **stars top-right, cash cows bottom-right, question marks top-left, dogs bottom-left**.

### Q.2.2 — Product-Oriented vs Market-Oriented Positioning (4 Marks)

(b) A coffee chain describes itself as "We sell coffee and snacks" while a competitor says "We deliver the third-place experience between home and work." Distinguish **product-oriented** from **market-oriented** positioning, explain why the market-oriented version is more defensible, and state the four components of a positioning statement.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

| Orientation | Focus | Definition style |
|-------------|-------|------------------|
| **Product-oriented** | *What we make / sell* | "We sell coffee and snacks" |
| **Market-oriented** | *Value, experience, benefit to the consumer* | "We deliver the third-place experience between home and work" |

**Why market-oriented is more defensible.** Product-oriented definitions invite comparison on specs and price — the brand becomes a commodity that can be undercut. Market-oriented positioning competes on **meaning and experience**: it builds an emotional connection, resists feature-based comparison, justifies premium pricing, and lets the brand *define the category* rather than fit into it. Starbucks wins on experience; Airbnb sells "belonging," not "renting places online"; LIC frames insurance as "financial support in life and after life."

**The four components of a positioning statement:**

1. **Target segment** — who is this for?
2. **Frame of reference** — what category do we compete in?
3. **Point of difference** — why choose us?
4. **Reason to believe** — what proof supports the claim?

**Pro tip:** Positioning questions love the *contrast-with-example* pattern. Give one product-oriented and one market-oriented version of the **same brand** (Starbucks/Walmart/Zoom are in the notes), then state why the market-oriented version resists commoditisation. If asked for the statement structure, list the four components (target → frame of reference → point of difference → reason to believe). Trap to avoid: positioning ≠ tagline — the tagline is the *expression*; positioning is the strategic mental space.

## Q.3

### Q.3.1 — The Four Ps and Seven Ps (4 Marks)

(a) Explain the difference between the classic **4Ps** and the extended **7Ps** of the marketing mix. Which three Ps are added, and why are they especially important for service businesses?

#### Answer

**The 4Ps** are the classic decision clusters for designing an offer:

| P | Answers the question | Includes |
|---|----------------------|----------|
| **Product** | What do we sell? | Features, quality, branding, UX, warranties |
| **Price** | What is the exchange? | Strategy, list price, discounts, payment methods, credit terms |
| **Place** | How do they get it? | Website, marketplaces, retail, wholesale, export channels |
| **Promotion** | How do they find out and want it? | Messaging, search, social, email, PR, partnerships |

**The extended 3Ps** — added for a complete, consistent experience:

| P | Meaning | Why it matters for services |
|---|---------|------------------------------|
| **People** | Who is hired and how they act | In services the *employee is part of the product* — behaviour, training, and culture directly shape the experience |
| **Process** | How delivery stays consistent | Services are produced and consumed simultaneously; standardisation, complaint handling, and response times determine consistency |
| **Physical evidence** | Tangible cues surrounding the experience | Services are intangible — the store, app UI, packaging, and ambience are the *proof* the service is real |

**Why the 7Ps matter together:** a strong product fails if people or process are poor, and premium branding collapses if physical evidence contradicts the promise (e.g., a dirty store). The 7Ps design a **consistent, consumer-focused experience**, which the 4Ps alone cannot fully deliver.

**Pro tip:** 4Ps-vs-7Ps is a listing question — do not stop at the names. For each added P give its *meaning* and one sentence on why services need it. The trap: forgetting **Physical evidence** in pure digital cases (the app UI, emails, and unboxing still count). If the case is service-heavy (hospitality, education, SaaS), lean hard on People/Process/Evidence — that is what the marks reward.

### Q.3.2 — Price Elasticity of Demand Calculation (6 Marks)

(b) A marketer raises the price of a product by 10% and observes that quantity demanded falls by 20%. Calculate the price elasticity of demand, classify it, and state what this implies for the revenue effect of the price increase. State any assumption made.

#### Answer

**Assumption (state it in the exam):** all other factors (income, competitor prices, preferences) remain constant — ceteris paribus — so the observed change is attributable to the price move alone.

**Formula.**

$$E_d = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}} = \frac{\Delta Q/Q}{\Delta P/P}$$

**Calculation.**

$$\Delta Q/Q = -20\%, \quad \Delta P/P = +10\%$$

$$E_d = \frac{-20\%}{+10\%} = -2$$

Taking the absolute value: $|E_d| = 2$.

**Classification.** Since $|E_d| = 2 > 1$, demand is **elastic** — consumers are highly sensitive to price (many substitutes, optional/aspirational product).

**Revenue implication.** For elastic demand, **raising price decreases total revenue**: the quantity drop (20%) proportionally outweighs the price gain (10%), so $R = P \times Q$ falls. Concretely, $P$ up 10% and $Q$ down 20% means total revenue declines (e.g., from $100 \times 50 = 5000$ to $110 \times 40 = 4400$). The correct strategic move is the **opposite**: for elastic demand, a *price decrease* grows revenue.

| Demand type | Raise price | Lower price |
|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| Elastic ($\|E_d\| > 1$) | Revenue **down** | Revenue **up** |
| Inelastic ($\|E_d\| < 1$) | Revenue **up** | Revenue **down** |
| Unitary ($\|E_d\| = 1$) | Revenue unchanged | Revenue unchanged |

**Pro tip:** Elasticity calculations are mark machines — show the formula, plug the numbers, and box the final answer. Three traps: (1) **writing the formula upside down** (numerator is %ΔQ, denominator is %ΔP), (2) forgetting the sign/absolute value, (3) concluding "raise price" when demand is elastic — that *lowers* revenue. If the question gives absolute numbers instead of percentages, compute the relative changes first: $\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}$ and $\frac{\Delta P}{P}$. Always anchor the answer to the revenue table; that is the strategic half of the marks.

## Q.4

### Q.4.1 — Blue Ocean vs Red Ocean Strategy (5 Marks)

(a) Contrast **red ocean** and **blue ocean** strategy. Define **value innovation** and explain why a blue ocean is not a permanent state.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

| | Red ocean | Blue ocean |
|---|-----------|------------|
| Market space | **Known/existing** industries | **Unexplored/new** market space |
| Demand | **Fight over existing** demand | **Create new** demand |
| Competitive goal | Outperform rivals, steal share | **Make competition irrelevant** |
| Value–cost logic | Choose differentiation **OR** low cost | **Value innovation** — pursue differentiation **AND** low cost |
| Nature over time | Crowded, profits pressured | Attracts imitators; eventually fills up |

**Value innovation** is the engine of blue ocean strategy: **raise buyer value (differentiation)** while **reducing cost structure simultaneously**, so the firm is not forced to pick one side of the classic trade-off. The alignment of both creates new demand and new market space (e.g., Cirque du Soleil combining theatre and spectacle — without animal acts — instead of out-clowning traditional circuses).

**Why blue oceans are not permanent.** The moment a blue ocean becomes visible and profitable, competitors notice and enter; the calm water fills up and turns red (early ride-hailing felt blue, then became a red ocean of competing apps). So the strategic implication is: **do not hunt for a permanent blue ocean** — keep innovating, refreshing value, and opening new spaces before rivals catch up.

**Pro tip:** Blue-ocean questions reward the contrast table plus the *value innovation* definition (both differentiation and low cost — not "premium only"). Two designed traps: equating blue ocean with "any innovation" (a minor feature update in a crowded category is not new market space), and claiming blue oceans last forever. If asked "which strategy is better?", say neither is morally superior — red-ocean tools (cost, differentiation, efficiency) are correct in mature, well-understood markets; blue ocean suits underserved jobs-to-be-done.

### Q.4.2 — Purchase Decision Involvement (5 Marks)

(b) Explain **low-involvement** and **high-involvement** purchase decisions. Identify the three problem-solving modes a consumer may use, and for each, state the corresponding marketing focus.

#### Answer

**The core link:** level of **involvement + perceived risk** determines how much effort the consumer puts into the decision.

| Dimension | Low involvement | High involvement |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Behavior | Routine / habitual | Extended problem-solving |
| Risk if wrong | Low | High |
| Price / stakes | Usually lower | Often higher price or life impact |
| Frequency | Often frequent | Infrequent |
| Example | Ordering the same Diet Coke at lunch | Car, house, insurance |

**Three problem-solving modes:**

| Mode | When it appears | Marketing focus |
|------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Routine problem-solving** | Brand loyalty / familiar, low-risk purchase | Habit cues, packaging recognition, shelf presence, reminders, **near-zero friction** |
| **Limited problem-solving** | Impulse / moderate situations, quick decision | Point-of-purchase, scarcity/offer triggers, simplify choice |
| **Extended problem-solving** | High risk or high uncertainty | Comparison content, proof, guarantees, demos, reviews, sales assistance |

**Rule of thumb:** involvement + perceived risk → decision effort. Routine response is *efficient*, not lazy — for low-involvement categories, brands that win loyalty *become* the routine. Conversely, underinvesting in proof for high-involvement purchases loses the sale.

**Pro tip:** The exam pattern is "involvement → which problem-solving mode → what marketing does." Use the three-mode table and always attach the *marketing focus* to each — that is where marks land. Traps: equating "cheap" alone with low involvement (familiarity and risk also matter), and calling every fast purchase "routine" (impulse often sits under **limited** problem-solving). Remember the canonical examples: Diet Coke at lunch = routine; car/house/insurance = extended.
