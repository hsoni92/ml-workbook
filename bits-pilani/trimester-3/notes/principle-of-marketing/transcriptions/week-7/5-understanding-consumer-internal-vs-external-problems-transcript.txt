Before we go further,
it helps to challenge a common idea
about consumer decision making. We
often assume consumers make decisions
through a series of logical steps
evaluating options, comparing features,
and following a rational path before
saying yes. But in reality, most buying
decisions happen emotionally. People
respond to feelings, intuition, and
quick emotional triggers rather than a
long analytical process. So this is
what we call how we think versus how we
buy. Understanding this gap between how
we think people buy and how they
actually buy helps marketeers design
messages that connect emotionally and
guide consumers more effectively toward
a yes. In order to understand this, we
have to understand there are two types
of problems a consumer usually faces.
One is called an external problem, and
another one is called internal problems.
External problems are like the visible
symptoms, the issues people show on the
surface, whereas internal problems go
much deeper. These are the real causes
behind the symptoms. They're often the
things people don't talk about because
of shame, embarrassment, unawareness,
or complexity. Understanding the
difference between internal and
external problems helps us understand
not just what people experience, but
why they experience it. Now let's look
at how we can map these consumer
problems. Customers often talk about
the external problem because it's
easier to express, but the real force
behind their decision is the internal
struggles that they're trying to
resolve. When you understand the deeper
emotional driver, the fear, the desire,
or the identity behind the surface, you
can speak what truly motivates them to
act. Let's take an example. If a
consumer says, I need a cool backpack,
it's kind of an external problem, but
for the backpack, the deeper need is to
express a conscious, authentic self.
It's something that is more functional,
tasteful, that aligns with the
artisanal values. So the internal
problem could be like. I need something
which is more functional, stylish, with
extra zippers, so on and so forth.
Again, let's take another example. The
external problem the consumer could be
relaying is, I want to get better at
drawing and painting, but the internal
problem that she's battling could be, I
want to become an artist, not just draw
better. I want to seek a long-term
creative identity and practise. And
this is how a company can design the
marketing communications once they
understand the internal problems.
Another example, a consumer might say,
I need a better design library, but the
internal problem could be my work life
is cluttered, it's a mess. I want to
fix some of the few things. Once the
company understands both the internal
and external problems and motivation of
the consumers, the company can design
effective marketing strategies. One
