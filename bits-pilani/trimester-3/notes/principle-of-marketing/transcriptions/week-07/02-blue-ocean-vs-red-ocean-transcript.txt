Before we move ahead, let's slow down
and look at a simple idea that changes
how we think about competition. Chan
King and Rainy Marin coined the terms
red and blue oceans to denote the
market universe. They denoted two key
concepts in the market universe a red
ocean and a blue ocean. What are red
oceans? Red oceans represent all the
industries in existence today the known
market space where industry boundaries
are clearly defined. In this market,
companies compete intensely to
outperform rivals, capture a larger
share of existing demand as competition
becomes increasingly cutthroat, profits
shrink, and market turns crowded,
giving rise to the term as red ocean.
Imagine an ocean where everyone is kind
of fighting with each other. On the
other hand, blue oceans represents
industries that don't yet exist, wide
open market spaces that are unexplored
and untouched by a large part of the
competition. Instead of fighting over
existing demand, they allow businesses
to create new value where no one else
is playing. Like the vast, deep and
powerful blue ocean, these spaces offer
immense opportunity and potential for
profitable growth. That traditional
markets simply cannot provide. Red
ocean represents existing markets where
competition is fierce. Companies fight
over the same consumers, leading to
limited growth and declining profits.
As we mentioned, on the contrast, blue
oceans refer to unexplored market
spaces with no direct competition. Here
companies unlock new demand,
create fresh opportunities, and
persuade profitable growth in an open
and untapped environment. Let's take a
deep dive to further understand how
strategies should be used and deployed
in a red ocean and blue ocean
environments. If a business is
operating on a red ocean, red ocean
strategies focus on competing with
existing markets. Businesses fight to
outperform one another, exploit the
demand that already exists, and choose
either between differentiation or low
cost. This often leads to crowded
competition and shrinking opportunities.
On the other hand, Blue Ocean
Strategies talks about creating an
entirely new market space where
competitions become irrelevant. Instead
of fighting for existing demand,
companies generate and capture new
demand. They break the traditional
value cost tradeoff and align all
activities towards achieving both
differentiation and low cost
simultaneously. In a Red Ocean
environment, companies focus on keeping
the costs low. And competing within the
cost differential model. The goal is to
perform better than rivals in an
already crowded market.
In contrast, Blue Ocean's strategy
shifts the focus toward creating more
value for the buyer. Instead of
fighting with competitors,
organisations adopt a value max model,
raising buyer value while unlocking new
demand and new market space. By
reducing cost on one side and
maximising value on the other side,
companies achieve value innovation and
break free from traditional competitive
boundaries. So the question that you
might be thinking is, is a blue ocean
ever permanent? The answer is no. Many
companies search for a blue ocean that
they can hold forever, an uncontested
space that stays untouched, but in
reality, no blue ocean remains
permanent. Once a new market is created
and its value becomes visible,
competitors start to enter. Over time,
even the calmest blue ocean begins to
fill up, turning it into a red ocean.
So the key is not to find a forever
blue ocean, but to continuously
innovate, refresh value, and stay ahead
by creating new spaces before others
catch up.
