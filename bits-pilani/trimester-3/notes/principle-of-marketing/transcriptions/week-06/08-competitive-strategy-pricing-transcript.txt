Customer lifetime value, or CLV, refers
to the total revenue a business can
expect from a consumer over the entire
duration of the relationship. It's a
forward-looking metric that helps
businesses evaluate not only how much a
consumer has already spent, but also
how much value that consumer is likely
to generate in the future. Instead of
focusing on one-time transactions, CLV
emphasises long-term engagement and
repeat purchases. To better understand
customer lifetime value, let's walk
through a simple example from a
subscription-based business. Imagine a
company offering a subscription product
priced at $50 per month. Now consider
three different types of customer.
First, we have cautious Claire. Claire
subscribes but cancels the first
delivery. Her lifetime value is $50 but
the business spent $70 to acquire her
and another $25 to serve her. That
means the company actually loses $45 on
this customer.
Next we have experimental Eric. Eric
stays slightly longer and cancels the
second payment. His lifetime value
becomes $100 but after accounting for
acquisition cost and two months of
product and shipping, the company still
loses $20. Finally, we have loyal Luke.
Luke continues his subscription for
five months or more, generating $250 or
more in lifetime value. After covering
acquisition and service costs, Luke
generates a profit of $55 and may
continue contributing revenue beyond
that. This example highlights not all
customers create the same value even if
they pay the same monthly price.
Ultimately, tracking customer lifetime
value helps businesses understand which
customers truly support long-term
growth and profitability. Now let's
look at how to calculate customer
lifetime value or CLV. The simplest way
to calculate CLV could be by
multiplying the average revenue per
consumer by the consumer lifespan and
then subtracting the total cost to
serve that consumer. This approach
works best when business has a reliable
historical data and consistent pricing
model, allowing it to better estimate
consumer value more accurately and
precisely.
For example, suppose a consumer spends
₹10,000 per year and stays with the
company for 5 years. In that case, the
gross CLV could be ₹50,000. If the
total cost to support and serve that
customer during the same period is
₹15,000 the net CLV will be $50,000
minus the 15,000, that is 35,000. Next,
to understand and improve customer
lifetime value, or CLV, businesses need
to track several key metrics that
influence it. One important metric is
average purchase value, which is
calculated by dividing total revenue by
the number of purchases. Next is
purchase frequency, which tells us how
often a consumer buys within a specific
time period. Another factor is consumer
lifespan or the average duration a
consumer's relationship with the
business. We could also monitor the
consumer churn rate, which represents
the percentage of consumers who stop
doing business with the company over a
point of time. Closely related to this
is consumer profitability score, which
compares the revenue earned from a
consumer with the cost required to
serve them. This is especially helpful
when comparing high touch and low touch
accounts. In digital subscription
business, user adoption is another
critical metric. It measures how many
users actively engage with the product
over time. Similarly, an engagement
score tracks signals such as logic,
event participation, and other
interactions that indicate ongoing
interest. Finally, businesses also look
at product expansion, which reflects
how consumers grow across different
solutions or offerings, not just how
much they spend. Together, these
metrics help organisations better
understand, predict, and improve
consumer lifetime value.
