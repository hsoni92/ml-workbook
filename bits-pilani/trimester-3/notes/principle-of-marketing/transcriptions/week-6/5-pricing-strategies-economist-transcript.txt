economist's perspective. Let's start
with price discrimination, a strategy
where seller charges different prices
for the same product depending on what
different group of buyers are willing
to pay. So first, let's look at first
degree or perfect price discrimination
strategy. This is often referred to as
the holy grail for economists. In this
strategy, the seller charges each
individual consumer the maximum price
they're willing to pay for a product or
service. When this happens, the result
is simple. Consumer surplus becomes
zero. All the value that consumers
would normally gain is captured
entirely by the producer. A classic
example is personalised haggling, like
negotiating prices at a flea market or
high-end legal services that adjust
their fees based on clients' financial
capacity. In today's world, a modern
version of this is seen in dynamic
pricing algorithms. The systems analyse
our browsing behaviour, purchase
history, and online patterns to present
you with a personalised price that may
be different from what others see. Next,
let's look at a situation where the
seller doesn't know what exactly each
consumer is willing to pay. Instead of
guessing, the seller creates a pricing
structure that allows customers to
self-select based on how much they
consume. Here's the logic the price per
unit changes depending on the quantity
purchased. The more you buy, the lower
the unit price becomes. A simple
example could be. Buy one for $10 get
the second for $5 or think about a bulk
buying at a wholesale store where
bigger packs cost less per unit. From
economists' perspective, this form of
pricing helps capture extra value from
high volume users, where consumers who
are willing to buy more in exchange of
a reduced cost per unit. This method is
a smart way for sellers to segment
buyers without the need to know their
exact willingness to pay.
Now let's move to the 3rd degree, or
segment based pricing discrimination
strategy. In this approach, the market
is divided into clear groups based on
certain characteristics such as age,
location, or consumer status. Each
segment behaves differently, so
business adjusts price accordingly. The
logic is straightforward. Different
groups have different levels of priced
elasticity of demand. Some segments are
more sensitive to price changes, while
others are willing to pay more because
they need the product more urgently. As
a result, sellers charge higher prices
to the groups with inelastic demand and
lower prices to the groups that are
more price sensitive. Common examples
include student discounts, senior
citizen movie tickets, and premium
pricing for business class airline
travellers who typically show more
inelastic demand. This strategy helps
businesses maximise revenue by aligning
prices with the specific needs and
behaviours of each consumer segment.
Next, let's look at two-part tariffs.
In this pricing strategy, the total
price is split into two components a
fixed fee that gives consumer access
and a variable usage fee based on how
much they consume. Here's how it works.
The fixed fee captures the consumer's
entry value, essentially the pricing of
getting in. Meanwhile, the usage fee
covers the cost of whatever they
actually use. A common example is a gym
membership. You might pay a monthly
membership fee just to access the gym
facility. Then additional charges for
personal training sessions or specialty
classes can incur. The next one is
bundled pricing. Bundling is a strategy
where two or more distinct products are
packaged together and sold as a single
combined offer. This approach boosts
the consumer's perceived value, while
businesses increase the volume of items
they sell. A classic example is
McDonald's Value Meal. Instead of
purchasing each item separately, the
burger, the fries, and the drink are
all bundled together at a lower overall
price. Bundling works well because
consumers feel they're getting more for
their money, and it encourages them to
buy additional items they might not
have purchased on their own. So finally,
let's look at discounts, also known as
tactical price discrimination. This
strategy uses discounts to separate
consumers based on how much they value
their time versus their money. For
example, coupons and rebates require
effort. Only price sensitive consumers
will spend clipping coupons or filling
out rebate forms. This lets businesses
charge higher prices. To time poor
consumers and lower prices, those who
are more price conscious. Another form
of this is seasonal or temporal pricing,
like hotels charging more during peak
season and lowering the hotel rates in
the offseason to keep the rooms filled
when demand drops.
