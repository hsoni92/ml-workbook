Hi again. In this video we are going to
explore one of the most important
concepts in economics known as price
elasticity of demand. Understanding how
sensitive consumers are to price
changes is essential for making smart
pricing decisions. By the end of this
lesson, you will understand how price
changes affect demand, revenue, and
overall business strategy, and why
elasticity plays a critical role in
maximising profits and predicting
consumer behaviour. Before we dive
deeper into elasticity, let's quickly
recap a few foundational concepts.
Remember price. Price is nothing, but
it refers to the amount charged for a
product or service. Remember demand?
Demand is nothing but a combination of
three factors need, ability, and
willingness to pay.
When these 3 things come together, a
customer must have the need for a
product, the ability to pay for it, and
the willingness to pay the price. Then
a demand generates. Elasticity is
nothing, but it refers to the degree to
which quantity demanded responds when
the price of a product changes.
Together, all these concepts help us
understand how pricing decisions could
influence consumer demand and overall
business revenue. Broadly, there are
three types of demand elasticity. First,
elastic demand. Second, inelastic
demand. And third, unitary elasticity.
First, let's consider elastic demand.
In this case, consumers are highly
sensitive to price changes. Even a
small increase in price can lead to a
large drop in the quantity demanded.
Because of this strong reaction,
increasing the price usually reduces
total revenue, while decreasing the
price tend to increase the total
revenue. Elastic demand is common for
goods or products with many substitutes,
such as a specific brand of bottled
water. Second, let's have a look into
inelastic demand here. Consumers are
less sensitive to price changes because
they need the product regardless of the
cost at that particular moment. Even if
the price increases significantly, the
quantity demanded falls off just
slightly or marginally. In this
situation, increasing the price
generally increases the total revenue.
Inelastic demand is often seen with
necessities like insulin or gasoline,
as well as additive products such as
tobacco or products that generally have
no substitutes. And finally, we have
unitary elasticity. In this case, the
percentage change in price is exactly
matched by the percentage change in
quantity demanded. As a result, total
revenue remains unchanged, even when
the price changes. This situation often
represents a balance point sometimes
considered as the sweet spot for
maximising revenue. An example can be
seen in products like petrol for
commuters with a fixed monthly fuel
budget, where spending remains
relatively constant even if the price
fluctuates here and there. OK, to
summarise, the revenue impact depends
on the type of demand. When demand is
elastic, that is, increasing the price
reduces revenue, while decreasing price
increases it. On the other hand, when
the demand is inelastic, increasing
price raises revenue while decreasing
price lowers it. And with unitary
elasticity, revenue remains unchanged
regardless of the price change.
