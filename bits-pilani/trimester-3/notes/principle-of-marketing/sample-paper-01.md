## Q.1

### Q.1.1 — Value Equation and Marketing Levers (6 Marks)

(a) What is the mathematical definition of the "Value Equation"? Briefly explain how a marketer can make this value ratio greater than 1 by either modifying the numerator or the denominator, providing one class example for each approach.

#### Answer

**Definition.** The Value Equation expresses **perceived value** as a ratio of what the consumer *gets* to what the consumer *gives up*:

$$\text{Perceived Value} = \frac{\text{Benefits}}{\text{Costs}}$$

- **Benefits (numerator):** features, quality, experience, status, convenience, emotional satisfaction.
- **Costs (denominator):** price, time, effort, mental energy, risk, opportunity cost.

**Interpretation.** A ratio $> 1$ means the consumer perceives a "great deal" — benefits clearly exceed costs — which drives satisfaction, loyalty, and positive word-of-mouth. A ratio $= 1$ is fair value ("got what I paid for") but builds no loyalty; a ratio $< 1$ signals "overpaid" and leads to dissatisfaction and churn.

**Two levers to push the ratio above 1.**

| Lever | Mechanism | Class example |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| **Increase the numerator (benefits)** | Add features, improve quality, add service, better experience | A smartwatch brand adds an extended warranty and premium fitness-tracking features without raising the price |
| **Decrease the denominator (costs)** | Lower price, discounts, financing, or remove friction (time/effort) | Amazon's one-click checkout cuts effort and time so the non-monetary cost of buying falls |

Both levers raise the same ratio — one by making the gain bigger, the other by making the sacrifice smaller. Crucially, value is **perceived and subjective**, so a ₹100 coffee can feel high-value when ambience and status are part of the benefit assessment.

**Pro tip:** Any value-equation question is answered in three fixed steps: (1) write the formula $\text{Value} = \text{Benefits}/\text{Costs}$, (2) state the interpretation of $> 1$, $= 1$, $< 1$, (3) give **one** example per lever — one that raises benefits, one that lowers costs. A mark of distinction is mentioning a **non-monetary** cost (time/effort) in the denominator, because most students only think of price. If the question asks about premium pricing, argue the brand must add *perceived* benefit surplus, not just features.

### Q.1.2 — Primary vs Secondary Market Research (4 Marks)

(b) Define "Primary Market Research" and "Secondary Market Research". Identify two distinct benefits of using Primary Market Research over Secondary Research.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|------|------------|----------|
| **Primary Market Research** | Data collected **firsthand** for the organisation's specific question | Surveys, interviews, focus groups, experiments, MVP tests |
| **Secondary Market Research** | Data pulled from **existing** external studies, reports, and databases | Industry reports, government census data, Google Trends, competitor websites |

**Two distinct benefits of Primary over Secondary research.**

1. **Ownership / exclusivity.** The insights are proprietary — competitors cannot access the same data. Secondary data is shared/public, so it gives no competitive edge.
2. **Relevance / specificity.** Primary research is designed around the organisation's exact business problem (own questions, methods, and analysis), whereas secondary data is broader and only partially customisable.

(Equally valid substitutes: **control** over question design and analysis, or **timeliness** — primary research reflects current market conditions while secondary data may be dated.)

**Note on best practice:** The two are complements, not rivals — secondary research gives a fast, cheap landscape view; primary research adds depth. But when the question asks for benefits of primary, lead with ownership and relevance.

**Pro tip:** "Primary vs secondary" is a favourite definitional question. Anchor your answer to the axis **firsthand-for-our-question (primary) vs already-existing (secondary)**. When asked for two benefits, pick the pair with the strongest contrast: *exclusivity* (competitors can't see it) and *tailoring* (built for your problem). Avoid listing "lower cost" or "faster" as primary benefits — those are secondary's strengths and will cost you marks.

## Q.2

### Q.2.1 — Multi-Brand vs New Brand Strategy (5 Marks)

(a) A consumer goods multinational company is planning to expand its product portfolio and is choosing between a Multi-Brand strategy and a New Brand strategy. Contrast these two options. Under what conditions is a "New Brand" strategy preferred over "Multi-Brand" and what is its primary risk?

#### Answer

**Contrast.** These two sit at different cells of the brand development grid (brand × category):

| Dimension | Multi-Brand | New Brand |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| Brand name | **New** | **New** |
| Category | **Existing** (company already competes in it) | **New** (company entering an unfamiliar category) |
| Logic | Capture more shelf space and serve multiple price segments within a known category | Clean slate free of old brand associations |
| Upside | Stronger category presence; segment-specific offers | Suits radical innovation; no baggage |
| Signature risk | **Internal competition** — own brands cannibalise each other for the same shopper | **Heavy investment** to build awareness, trust, and presence from zero |

**When "New Brand" is preferred over "Multi-Brand":**

- The company needs a **radical break** — a genuinely new product that doesn't fit the parent brand's image.
- The existing brand carries **toxic or mismatched associations** that would hurt the new offering.
- The expansion moves into a **completely new category** where the company has no equity to lend.

Multi-brand, by contrast, is the choice when the company *stays* in a category it already plays in and simply needs more shelf space or distinct price tiers (e.g., Unilever's many shampoo brands).

**Primary risk of New Brand:** a heavy, uncertain spend to build **awareness, trust, and distribution presence** from nothing — unlike a line/brand extension that borrows existing equity, a new brand must earn recognition entirely from scratch.

**Pro tip:** The instant a question mentions "Multi-Brand" vs "New Brand", draw the 2×2 in your head: **brand (existing/new) × category (existing/new)**. Line extension = same/same, brand extension = same/new, multi-brand = new/same, new brand = new/new. The classic trap is calling Unilever's multiple shampoos a "line extension" — same category with *new brand names* is **multi-brand**. Always pair each strategy with its signature risk: cannibalization (line), dilution (brand extension), internal rivalry (multi-brand), high build cost (new brand). For the "when is it preferred" part, the keyword to echo is **clean slate**.

### Q.2.2 — First-Degree and Third-Degree Price Discrimination (5 Marks)

(b) Economists define Price Discrimination as the practice of charging different prices to different customers for the same product. Explain First-Degree (Perfect) Price Discrimination and Third-Degree (Segment-Based) Price Discrimination. Based on your explanations, justify why student discounts or senior citizen movie tickets are categorized as Third-Degree Price Discrimination.

#### Answer

**Price discrimination** is charging different prices for the **same** product based on what different buyers are willing to pay — the seller captures a larger share of consumer surplus.

**First-Degree (Perfect) Price Discrimination.** Charge **each individual consumer exactly their maximum willingness to pay (WTP)**. The seller needs to know each buyer's personal WTP, so every transaction is priced individually.

| Result | Implication |
|--------|-------------|
| Consumer surplus $\rightarrow 0$ | Producer captures *all* surplus |

**Examples:** personalised haggling at a flea market; dynamic pricing algorithms that personalise price from browsing history and behaviour. It is the economist's "holy grail" — powerful in theory, hard to achieve perfectly in practice.

**Third-Degree (Segment-Based) Price Discrimination.** The seller does **not** price per person; instead, it divides the market by **observable traits** (age, location, status) into segments that differ in **price elasticity**, and charges each segment accordingly.

| Segment elasticity | Price |
|--------------------|-------|
| More inelastic (urgent need / low price sensitivity) | **Higher** price |
| More elastic (price-sensitive) | **Lower** price |

**Examples:** business-class airfares (inelastic travellers — urgency, employer pays) vs economy (elastic); student discounts; senior citizen tickets.

**Why student/senior discounts are Third-Degree.** The price difference is not decided per individual (that would be first-degree) but by an **observable, easily verifiable segment trait** — student status or age. Students and seniors typically have **lower willingness to pay / higher price elasticity** for entertainment (limited disposable income, many substitute activities). Charging them a lower ticket price is the textbook third-degree move: same product (same movie), segmented pricing by an observable characteristic, with the lower price aimed at the more elastic segment. The customers self-identify via their student/senior card, so the seller can enforce the segmentation cheaply.

**Pro tip:** Price-discrimination questions are graded on **degree discipline** — never blur the three. First degree = individual WTP (consumer surplus $\to 0$); second degree = self-selection by quantity/version (unit price falls with volume); third degree = segment by observable traits, price by **elasticity** (high price for inelastic groups, low for elastic). For any "why is this third-degree?" justification, structure it as: (1) the segment is identified by an observable trait, (2) that segment has different elasticity, (3) the same product is sold at a different price to it. Also be ready to state the trap: a coupon is *not* first-degree — it is tactical screening (time vs money).

## Q.3

### Q.3.1 — B2C vs B2B Marketing Dynamics (4 Marks)

(a) Companies must adapt their marketing strategies based on whether they sell directly to consumers (B2C) or to other businesses (B2B). Contrast B2C and B2B marketing dynamics by comparing them across three specific areas: Primary Driver, Decision Logic, and Risk Factor.

#### Answer

| Area | B2C (business to consumer) | B2B (business to business) |
|------|----------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Primary Driver** | **Personal wants, needs, and emotions** — individual preference, status, convenience, impulse | **Rational business value** — ROI, cost savings, productivity, solving an organisational problem |
| **Decision Logic** | Simpler, often **single individual**, faster and less formal; smaller purchase amounts and lower commitment | **Multi-stakeholder** buying committee (users, budget holder, procurement, sometimes IT/legal); formal evaluation, RFPs, longer sales cycle |
| **Risk Factor** | **Personal money, low stakes per purchase** — a bad choice costs the individual little; switching is easy | **High stakes and professional accountability** — large contracts, business impact, contractual/legal exposure; buyers face career risk if the choice fails |

**Implication for strategy:** B2C marketing leans on emotional messaging, mass reach, branding, and making buying easy and delightful (lower effort costs); B2B marketing leans on proof (case studies, demos, ROI calculators), relationship building, and serving multiple decision-makers in a long cycle. The core marketing logic is the same — create value and build relationships — but the *audience, message, and buying process* change.

**Pro tip:** Whenever a B2C-vs-B2B contrast is asked, use a **table with the exact dimensions named in the question** (here: Primary Driver, Decision Logic, Risk Factor) — examiners award marks for hitting all three. Keep the contrast on one crisp axis per row: emotion vs rationality, individual vs committee, personal/low stakes vs professional/high stakes. A strong closing line: "same core logic of value creation, different decision environment." Remember B2B churn targets are typically tighter (~5% monthly) than B2C (~7–10%) — a nice tie-in if the question drifts toward retention.

### Q.3.2 — The Four Relationship Groups (6 Marks)

(b) Within the customer relationship framework, marketers evaluate customers based on projected loyalty and potential profitability. Identify and define the four relational groups (Strangers, Butterflies, True Friends, and Barnacles) resulting from this 2x2 model. For each group, state the specific strategic action a company should take to manage the relationship.

#### Answer

**Framework.** The 2×2 relationship matrix is built on two axes: **projected loyalty** and **potential profitability**. The four cells define how much a company should invest in each customer type — and the strategic rule is *allocate resources to match the profile*, not treat all customers equally.

| Group | Profitability | Loyalty | Strategic action |
|-------|---------------|---------|------------------|
| **Strangers** | Low | Low | **Minimal investment**; maximise per-transaction revenue; treat as one-time customers, don't chase conversion |
| **Butterflies** | High | Low | **Short-term value capture** — enjoy them while they last; timed promotions and deals to maximise spend during the window; don't expect long-term commitment |
| **True Friends** | High | High | **Heavy long-term investment** — personalised experiences, loyalty programs, exclusive access; reward advocacy and referrals |
| **Barnacles** | Low | High | **Improve profitability** — upsell, cross-sell, premium tiers; raise average transaction value; avoid over-serving free value that drains resources |

**Detail worth writing.**

- **Strangers:** not worth relationship-building spend — welcome repeat visits, but focus on efficient, profitable one-off transactions (e.g., a tourist buying once at a convenience store).
- **Butterflies:** profitable but transient — they shop around by nature, so conversion efforts usually fail; the play is capturing their value while they're here (e.g., a trader switching between brokerage apps for the best deal).
- **True Friends:** the ideal segment — repeat purchases, positive referrals, resilience to competitor offers; invest the most (CRM, loyalty, personalisation) because they become brand advocates.
- **Barnacles:** loyalty without margin is a drain — keep them but raise their value through upselling, or they consume resources disproportionate to what they contribute (e.g., a bank customer making frequent small low-margin transactions).

**Pro tip:** A relationship-groups question is a 4-row table waiting to be written. For each group give **three things**: (profitability, loyalty) values, a one-line definition, and the **specific action**. The action must be directional — "invest heavily" for true friends, "maximise per-transaction revenue" for strangers, "short-term promotions" for butterflies, "upsell to improve margin" for barnacles. Two traps to avoid: expecting butterflies to become loyal (they're transient by design) and assuming high spend equals high profitability (barnacles spend often but on low margin). If asked to place them on a 2×2, remember True Friends = high/high, Barnacles = low/high, Butterflies = high/low, Strangers = low/low.

## Q.4

### Q.4.1 — Segmentation vs Targeting in the STP Framework (4 Marks)

(a) Differentiate between the "Segmentation" and "Targeting" steps of the STP framework. State and briefly explain any two criteria (such as geographic or demographic) a marketer can use to segment a consumer market.

#### Answer

**STP = Segmentation + Targeting → Positioning.** The two steps are sequential and distinct:

| Step | Definition | Key question |
|------|------------|--------------|
| **Segmentation** | Divide the broad market into **distinct subgroups with shared characteristics** | Who are the meaningful groups? |
| **Targeting** | **Evaluate and select** the segment(s) the company will pursue | Which groups are worth pursuing? |

Segmentation is the *act of dividing* the market into candidate groups; targeting is the *act of choosing* among those groups based on size, profitability, reachability, and fit. You segment first (map the landscape), then target (decide where to compete).

**Two segmentation criteria (any two of the following):**

| Criterion | Variables | Use case |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **Geographic** | Country, region, city, climate | Localised products and regional campaigns |
| **Demographic** | Age, gender, income, profession | Mass-market products with clear buyer profiles |
| **Behavioural** | Purchase patterns, channel preference, loyalty level | Retention programs, channel strategy |
| **Psychographic** | Lifestyle, values, hobbies, personality | Premium brands, identity-driven products |

**Example of why it matters:** the plant-based milk category splits into a *lifestyle-driven* segment (high-income, sustainability-motivated) and a *lactose-intolerant* segment (health necessity) — the same product needs different messages for each. Segmentation uncovers these groups; targeting decides which one to build for.

**Pro tip:** Keep the differentiation sharp: **segmentation divides, targeting selects.** The easiest way to write it is "Segmentation asks *who could buy?*; targeting asks *who should we serve?*". For the criteria, name **four bases** (geographic, demographic, behavioural, psychographic) and then expand the two the examiner asked for with a variable + use-case line each — showing a use case is what earns the mark. A bonus point: mention that targeting strategies can be *undifferentiated* (one message for all), *differentiated* (separate offers per segment), or *concentrated* (one niche).

### Q.4.2 — CAC and Payback Period Calculation (6 Marks)

(b) Define "Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)" and "Payback Period". If a business has a CAC of $300, an average monthly revenue per customer of $50, and a gross margin of 80%, calculate the payback period in months and briefly explain what this final number signifies.

#### Answer

**Definitions.**

- **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC):** the total sales-and-marketing spend required to acquire one new customer — the "price tag" on every new customer.

$$\text{CAC} = \frac{\text{Total sales and marketing expenses}}{\text{Number of new customers acquired}}$$

- **Payback Period:** the time needed to recover the CAC in cash — i.e., how many months of customer contribution it takes to earn back what was spent to acquire them.

$$\text{Payback period (months)} = \frac{\text{CAC}}{\text{Average monthly revenue per customer} \times \text{Gross margin}}$$

**Calculation.**

| Input | Value |
|-------|-------|
| CAC | $300 |
| Average monthly revenue per customer (AMR) | $50 |
| Gross margin (GM) | 80% = 0.8 |

$$\text{Monthly contribution} = AMR \times GM = \$50 \times 0.8 = \$40 \text{ per customer per month}$$

$$\text{Payback period} = \frac{\text{CAC}}{\text{Monthly contribution}} = \frac{\$300}{\$40} = 7.5 \text{ months}$$

**What it signifies.** The business recovers its $300 acquisition cost in **7.5 months** of gross-margin contribution from that customer. Only the margin — not raw revenue — pays back acquisition, which is why gross margin must enter the denominator. A 7.5-month payback is a **moderate, workable figure**: cash returns in under a year, which supports reinvestment, provided customer retention (churn) keeps the average lifetime long enough to repay CAC and then generate profit beyond month 7.5. If churn is high or the customer's lifetime is shorter than 7.5 months, the account is unprofitable regardless of the headline LTV.

**Pro tip:** This is a pure calculation question — show every step and box the final answer. The three traps examiners set: (1) forgetting gross margin (dividing by $50 → 6 months, wrong), (2) mixing decimals ($80\%$ as $80$ instead of $0.8$), (3) ignoring units (the answer is **months**, not rupees). Always anchor the *significance* line to cash-flow timing: short payback → fast cash return → reinvestment loop; long payback → cash-flow gap (ads are due in 30 days, recovery takes months). Bonus tie-ins if you want to impress: compare against the ~3:1 LTV:CAC benchmark and mention churn as the enemy of the payback working out.
